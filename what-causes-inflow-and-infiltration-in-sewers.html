What Causes Inflow and Infiltration in Sewers

By the Editorial Team | Published: July 2026 | Last Updated: July 2026

What Actually Drives Inflow and Infiltration in a Sewer System

Inflow and infiltration come from defects and connections that let clean water into a pipe built only for wastewater. Infiltration is groundwater entering through cracks, joints, and porous pipe below the water table. Inflow is stormwater entering through direct paths such as manhole covers and cross-connections. Knowing which sources dominate a given system is the first step in fixing it, because the two are found and sealed differently. This page walks through the causes in order of how much flow they usually contribute.

Why Pipe Joints and Aging Pipe Leak

Pipe joints are among the most common infiltration paths because they move as the ground around them shifts. Older clay and concrete sewers were laid in short sections with jointed connections that open as expansive soils swell and shrink. Once a joint separates even slightly, groundwater under pressure finds the gap and flows in continuously. Porous or corroded pipe barrel adds to the load in the oldest segments. The result is steady infiltration that rises whenever the water table climbs.

Why Service Laterals Are Often the Biggest Source

Service laterals are frequently the single largest source of infiltration because there are so many of them and their connections are often poor. Each property connection is a potential leak at the point where the lateral ties into the main. Many laterals are privately owned, older than the main, and never inspected, so defects accumulate unseen. Because a system can have thousands of laterals, their combined infiltration often exceeds that of the mainline itself. Any serious I&I program has to account for laterals, not just the main.

  • The connection point where the lateral meets the main is a frequent leak.
  • Cracked or root-damaged lateral pipe admits groundwater along its length.
  • Private ownership means many laterals are never inspected or maintained.
  • Sheer count makes laterals a dominant source even when each leaks a little.

Taken together, these traits are why laterals so often top the infiltration ranking. A program that seals the mainline but skips laterals leaves its largest single source untouched.

How Manholes and Structures Contribute

Manholes and lift stations add both inflow and infiltration through several distinct paths. The frame-to-cover seal and the chimney at the top admit stormwater as inflow. The walls and bench admit groundwater as infiltration where joints and penetrations have failed. Pipe penetrations through the structure wall leave annular gaps that leak. Because a manhole sits at every junction, small leaks at each one add up across a system.

How the Water Table Changes the Picture

Groundwater depth sets how hard infiltration pushes and how continuous it is. Where the water table is deep, a joint defect may leak only seasonally. Where it is shallow, as on much of the Gulf Coast, the same defect leaks year-round under constant pressure. Rainfall raises the water table and adds rainfall-derived infiltration on top of the baseline. Reading the local groundwater condition is part of predicting how much a given defect actually contributes.

Where Direct Inflow Enters the System

Inflow enters through direct connections to the surface rather than through soil, so its sources differ from infiltration. Unlike groundwater seepage, inflow arrives fast during rain and disappears when the storm passes. Finding these connections is usually quick with smoke testing, which pushes visible smoke through the pipe to reveal open paths at the surface. The common inflow points are worth knowing because closing them removes the sharpest wet-weather peaks.

  • Manhole covers and chimneys that let rainwater in from the street.
  • Roof downspouts or yard drains cross-connected to the sanitary sewer.
  • Foundation and area drains tied into the wrong system.
  • Cleanouts and abandoned connections left open at the surface.

Closing these direct connections is usually fast and removes the sharpest wet-weather peaks. Because inflow spikes during storms, sealing it often produces the most visible drop in overflow risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common source of infiltration?

Service laterals and pipe joints are the most common infiltration sources in aging systems. Laterals dominate in many systems because they are numerous and often poorly connected. Joints leak as the ground moves and the pipe ages. The exact ranking is confirmed by field investigation, but laterals and joints are almost always near the top.

How do I know if my problem is inflow or infiltration?

Flow patterns reveal which one dominates: infiltration is steady and tracks the water table, while inflow spikes with rainfall. Comparing dry-weather and wet-weather flow separates the two. Smoke testing quickly identifies inflow paths, and dyed-water testing and CCTV locate infiltration. Most systems have both, and the investigation quantifies the split.

Do tree roots cause infiltration?

Yes, roots both cause and follow infiltration because they grow toward the moisture leaking from a defect. A root intrusion at a joint or lateral widens the opening and admits more groundwater. Removing roots without sealing the defect only invites regrowth. Sealing the joint or connection is what removes the water the roots were seeking.

Can new pipe still have I&I?

Yes, even newer systems develop I&I through poor lateral connections, joint movement, and manhole defects. Construction defects and settlement can open paths early in a pipe's life. Ground movement in expansive clay stresses joints regardless of pipe age. New pipe reduces but does not eliminate the need for inspection and sealing.

Why do manholes leak so much?

Manholes leak because they combine several vulnerable paths in one structure: the cover, chimney, walls, and pipe penetrations. The top admits stormwater as inflow, while the walls and penetrations admit groundwater as infiltration. Because a manhole sits at every junction, the system has many of them. Small leaks at each add up to a meaningful total.

Does rainfall change how much infiltration enters?

Yes, rainfall raises the water table and adds rainfall-derived infiltration on top of the steady baseline. A joint that leaks slowly in dry weather can leak much faster after the ground saturates. This is why infiltration is worst in wet seasons even though it is not storm inflow. Investigations account for antecedent rainfall when quantifying infiltration.

Are private laterals the system owner's responsibility?

Ownership of laterals varies by jurisdiction, and it strongly affects how a program is scoped. In many places the property owner owns the lateral, which complicates access and funding for repairs. Because laterals are often a dominant infiltration source, programs that ignore them leave much of the problem unaddressed. Clarifying ownership early shapes the whole reduction strategy.
