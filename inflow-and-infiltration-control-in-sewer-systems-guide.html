Inflow and Infiltration Control in Sewer Systems Guide

How Inflow and Infiltration Quietly Overloads a Sewer System

Inflow and infiltration, together called I&I, is clean groundwater and stormwater leaking into a sanitary sewer that was only sized to carry wastewater. Every gallon of that clean water takes capacity a collection system needs for actual sewage. On the Texas-Louisiana Gulf Coast, where the water table sits close to the surface for most of the year, infiltration runs high and steady rather than only after storms. Left unaddressed, I&I drives sanitary sewer overflows, inflates treatment costs, and forces premature capacity upgrades. This guide explains what I&I is, where it enters, how it is sealed, and how a reduction program is planned and verified for Gulf Coast collection systems.

What Inflow and Infiltration Actually Are

Infiltration and inflow are two different problems that arrive through two different paths. Infiltration is groundwater seeping into the pipe through defects below the water table, so it is steady and worst where the ground stays saturated. Inflow is surface water entering through direct connections such as manhole covers, downspouts, or cross-connections, so it spikes during rain. Telling them apart matters because each is found and fixed differently.

The practical takeaway is that a system almost always carries both at once, in shifting proportions. Separating the two during investigation is what lets each be routed to the repair that actually stops it.

Where Groundwater Enters a Collection System

Most infiltration enters through a short list of predictable defects, and the same list drives where sealing work is aimed. Aging clay and concrete pipe develops cracked and offset joints as the ground moves. Service laterals and their connections to the main are among the largest single sources in many systems. Manholes and lift stations leak through walls, chimneys, and pipe penetrations. Typical entry points include:

  • Cracked or offset pipe joints in mainlines below the water table.
  • Failed lateral connections where a service line ties into the main.
  • Leaking manhole walls, chimneys, and frame-to-cover seals.
  • Pipe penetrations and annular spaces at structure walls.
  • Porous or deteriorated pipe barrel in older segments.

These entry points repeat from one system to the next, so a crew knows where to look before the first inspection. Ranking which of them dominates a specific network is the job of a field investigation.

How I&I Is Reduced Without Digging Up the Street

Infiltration is reduced with trenchless methods that seal defects from inside the pipe rather than by excavation. Chemical grouting is injected through packers to seal joints, laterals, and structures, forming a barrier in the surrounding soil so groundwater can no longer reach the defect. Cured-in-place pipe (CIPP) lining rebuilds the pipe barrel structurally, and coatings restore manhole walls. These methods are complementary: lining rebuilds the pipe while grouting seals the joints, laterals, and penetrations that lining alone can bridge but not seal. The mechanism behind the grouting side is covered in depth in the reference on how grouting reduces inflow and infiltration in sewer systems.

Why Reducing I&I Pays Off

Cutting I&I frees hydraulic capacity, lowers pumping and treatment costs, and defers expensive plant expansions. Every gallon of groundwater removed from the pipe is a gallon the treatment plant no longer has to move and process. Reducing peak flows also lowers the risk of sanitary sewer overflows, which carry regulatory and cleanup consequences. In fast-growing Gulf Coast systems, sealing I&I can recover enough capacity to serve new connections without building new infrastructure. Regulators increasingly tie overflow reduction to consent decrees, so a documented reduction program can also satisfy a compliance obligation while it saves money. The payback is measured in avoided overflow penalties, lower energy bills, deferred capital spending, and a clearer regulatory record.

How an I&I Reduction Program Works

A sound program follows a repeatable order: find the water, rank the defects, seal them, and verify the drop in flow. Skipping the investigation and jumping straight to repairs is the most common way budgets are wasted on the wrong pipes. The stages below describe how a measurable program is built.

  • Monitor flow to quantify how much I&I the system carries and where.
  • Inspect with CCTV, smoke testing, and dyed-water testing to locate defects.
  • Rank defects by how much infiltration each contributes per dollar to fix.
  • Seal the highest-value defects with grouting, lining, or structure repair.
  • Re-monitor flow to confirm the reduction and document it for a capacity study.

Each stage feeds the next, and the final flow monitoring is what proves the money was well spent. A program that seals defects without measuring the result cannot show a capacity gain. Treating investigation, repair, and verification as one connected effort is what produces defensible numbers.

How Gulf Coast Conditions Change an I&I Program

Shallow groundwater and expansive clay make infiltration a year-round load on Gulf Coast collection systems rather than a seasonal one. A high water table keeps constant pressure on every buried joint, so defects leak continuously instead of only after storms. Expansive clay swells and shrinks with the seasons, opening new joints and cracking manhole benches over time. These conditions raise both the baseline infiltration and the rate at which new defects appear. Reading the local groundwater and soil behavior is part of sizing a realistic program.

  • A shallow water table drives steady, year-round infiltration, not just wet-weather spikes.
  • Expansive clay opens joints and cracks structures as it cycles through wet and dry seasons.
  • New defects appear faster, so investigation and sealing become ongoing rather than one-time.
  • Programs are sized for continuous infiltration, not the lower baseline of drier regions.

Because the ground keeps working against the pipe, a Gulf Coast program treats I&I control as maintained rather than finished. Sealing today's sources lowers flow now, while periodic monitoring catches the next generation of leaks. Communities across the Houston area and Harris County face the same pattern, so a program built for one system usually transfers to its neighbors. Building for continuous conditions is what keeps flows down over the long term.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between inflow and infiltration?

Infiltration is groundwater seeping into the sewer through defects, while inflow is surface water entering through direct connections. Infiltration is steady and worst when the water table is high, so it dominates in wet-ground regions like the Gulf Coast. Inflow spikes during rain because it comes from stormwater paths such as manhole covers and cross-connections. The two are separated during investigation because each is found and fixed by different methods.

Why is I&I a bigger problem on the Gulf Coast?

A persistently high water table keeps groundwater pressed against buried pipe for most of the year, so infiltration is nearly constant rather than seasonal. Shallow groundwater means even small joint defects leak continuously. Expansive clay soils also open joints as they move, creating new paths over time. Together these conditions make infiltration a year-round load on Gulf Coast collection systems rather than an occasional one.

Does reducing I&I actually save money?

Yes, because every gallon of clean water kept out of the sewer is a gallon the system does not pump, convey, or treat. Lower peak flows reduce the risk of overflows and the penalties that follow them. Recovered capacity can defer or shrink treatment-plant expansions that would otherwise be needed for growth. The savings show up in energy, avoided capital spending, and reduced overflow exposure.

Which sources contribute the most infiltration?

Service laterals and pipe joints are usually the largest infiltration sources in older collection systems, followed by manholes. Laterals are numerous and often poorly connected, so together they can dominate a system's infiltration. Joints leak as the ground moves and the pipe ages. Manholes add inflow and infiltration through walls, chimneys, and covers. A field investigation ranks the actual sources for a given system.

Can I&I be fixed without excavation?

Yes, most I&I is addressed with trenchless methods that work from inside the pipe. Chemical grouting is injected through packers to seal joints and laterals, and cured-in-place lining rebuilds the pipe barrel without digging. Manholes are sealed with coatings and grouting. Excavation is reserved for defects too severe for trenchless repair, which keeps most of a program non-disruptive.

How is the success of an I&I program measured?

Success is measured by comparing flow before and after the repairs, ideally under similar groundwater and rainfall conditions. Flow monitors quantify the reduction in gallons per day, which is the number a capacity study relies on. Rainfall-derived inflow can also be tracked to confirm inflow paths were closed. Documented before-and-after flow data is what turns a repair program into a defensible capacity gain.

Does grouting replace pipe lining, or work with it?

Grouting and lining work together rather than competing, because they address different defects. Cured-in-place lining rebuilds the structural pipe barrel, while grouting seals the joints, laterals, and penetrations that a liner bridges but does not seal. On a lined mainline, joints and lateral connections can still leak and are sealed by grouting. Most thorough programs use both methods in sequence.
