Sealing Manholes and Lift Stations Against Infiltration

By the Editorial Team | Published: July 2026 | Last Updated: July 2026

Why Manholes and Lift Stations Leak So Much Groundwater

Manholes and lift stations are concentrated sources of inflow and infiltration because they combine several leak paths in one structure. The top of a manhole admits stormwater as inflow through the cover and chimney. The walls, bench, and pipe penetrations admit groundwater as infiltration. Because a collection system has a manhole at nearly every junction, small leaks at each one add up to a large total. This page explains where these structures leak and how each path is sealed.

Where a Manhole Actually Leaks

A manhole leaks through a predictable set of locations from the cover down to the invert. The frame-to-cover seal and the chimney section at the top are classic inflow paths during rain. The wall joints and the bench admit groundwater where mortar or seals have failed. The pipe penetrations through the wall leave annular gaps that leak under groundwater pressure. Knowing these locations lets a crew target sealing rather than guess.

  • Frame-to-cover seal and chimney: stormwater inflow at the top.
  • Wall joints and precast section seams: groundwater infiltration.
  • Pipe penetrations and annular spaces: infiltration at the invert level.
  • Bench and floor cracks: infiltration where the structure has moved.

Knowing these locations turns manhole repair into targeted work rather than guesswork. Each path has a matching sealing method, which the next section lays out in order.

How Each Path Is Sealed

Each leak path is matched to a sealing method, and infiltration is stopped before any protective coating goes on. Chimney and frame seals are addressed with flexible seals that tolerate cover movement. Wall and penetration infiltration is stopped with chemical grouting injected into the surrounding soil. Once the structure is dry, a spray-applied lining or coating restores and protects the interior. The order matters: sealing the water first is what lets the coating bond and last.

Frame-to-cover and chimneyInflowFlexible chimney seal

The order in the table matters as much as the methods themselves. Stopping infiltration before any coating goes on is what lets the finished repair bond and last.

Why Lift Stations Need Extra Attention

Lift stations concentrate flow and equipment, so infiltration there carries a higher cost than at an ordinary manhole. Every gallon of groundwater that enters a lift station is pumped, which raises energy use and wear on the pumps. Standing groundwater around the wet well also accelerates corrosion of the structure and equipment. Because a lift station is a critical node, keeping it sealed protects both capacity and expensive hardware. Sealing the wet well and its penetrations is a high-value target in any program.

Why Gulf Coast Conditions Raise the Stakes

A shallow water table keeps groundwater pressed against manhole walls and wet wells for most of the year. Constant pressure means a small wall or penetration defect leaks continuously rather than seasonally. Expansive clay movement also cracks benches and opens penetrations over time. In this setting, manhole and lift-station sealing is not a one-time fix but part of an ongoing infiltration-control strategy. Reading the local groundwater condition guides how aggressively these structures are sealed.

What a Manhole Inspection Checks

A manhole inspection works top to bottom so no leak path is missed, and it records condition in a way repairs can be planned from. The inspector notes the cover and chimney, the walls and seams, the pipe penetrations, and the bench and invert. Documenting each location by condition lets a program rank which structures to seal first. The checklist below covers what a thorough inspection captures.

  • Cover and chimney condition, and any visible stormwater inflow.
  • Wall joints, seams, and active groundwater infiltration.
  • Pipe penetrations and annular gaps at the structure wall.
  • Bench and invert cracks, and evidence of past movement.

A top-to-bottom record like this is what lets a program rank which structures to seal first. Consistent inspection notes turn a set of manholes into a prioritized repair list.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are manholes such a big source of I&I?

Manholes combine several leak paths, the cover, chimney, walls, and penetrations, in one structure, and there are many of them. The top admits stormwater inflow while the walls and penetrations admit groundwater infiltration. Because a manhole sits at nearly every junction, small leaks at each add up across the system. Their sheer number makes them a major contributor even when each leaks modestly.

What is a chimney seal?

A chimney seal is a flexible seal installed at the top of a manhole to close the frame-to-cover and chimney area against inflow. It tolerates the movement of the cover and the adjustment ring without cracking. Because the chimney is a classic stormwater inflow path, sealing it removes a common wet-weather source. It is one of the most cost-effective manhole repairs.

Do you have to stop the leak before coating a manhole?

Yes, infiltration must be stopped first because a coating will not bond to a wet, actively leaking surface. Grouting stops the groundwater so the wall can dry. The coating then restores and protects the interior. Coating over an active leak is a common cause of early failure, which is why sealing comes first.

How is groundwater sealed out of a manhole wall?

Groundwater is sealed out with chemical grouting injected into the soil behind the wall, forming a barrier before the water reaches the structure. The grout cures in the surrounding ground and blocks the infiltration path. This soil-side seal addresses the water at its source rather than just the visible wall face. It is the same principle used to seal pipe joints and penetrations.

Why does infiltration cost more at a lift station?

Infiltration at a lift station is more costly because every gallon that enters is pumped, adding energy use and pump wear. Groundwater around the wet well also accelerates corrosion of the structure and equipment. As a critical node, a leaking lift station puts both capacity and hardware at risk. Sealing it protects more than just hydraulic capacity.

How often should manholes be inspected for leaks?

Inspection frequency depends on age, groundwater, and past performance, but high-water-table systems warrant closer attention. Manholes in shallow-groundwater areas leak continuously, so defects there matter more. Routine inspection catches new cracks and seal failures before they grow. A risk-based schedule focuses inspection on the structures most likely to leak.

Can manhole sealing be done without shutting down flow?

Often yes, because much manhole sealing is done within the structure without stopping the sewer. Chimney seals and interior grouting can proceed around normal flow in many cases. Heavier rehabilitation may require flow control or bypass, depending on the structure. The approach is matched to the structure and the flow it carries.
